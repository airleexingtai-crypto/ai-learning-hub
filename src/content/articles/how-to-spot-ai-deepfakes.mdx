This guide teaches you how to spot fake videos (check the eyes, ears, and lighting), cloned voices (listen for flat emotion and breathing gaps), and AI-generated images (look at the hands and text). ## What are deepfakes and why they matter in 2026

A deepfake is any media (video, audio, or image) that AI has manipulated or entirely generated to look or sound like a real person. The term first appeared around 2017 on Reddit. Back then, the output was glitchy and easy to spot. Not anymore.

In 2026, a teenager with a decent laptop can generate a convincing face-swap video in under ten minutes. Voice cloning tools need only 30 seconds of sample audio to produce a near-perfect replica of someone's voice. The barrier to entry has collapsed.

This matters because deepfakes are now the fastest-growing category of fraud. The FBI reported that losses tied to deepfake scams in the US exceeded $3.6 billion in 2025. Globally, the number is much higher. Political deepfakes have appeared in elections across Europe, Asia, and South America. And if you have a social media presence at all, your face and voice are already in training datasets somewhere.

The good news: most deepfakes still have telltale flaws. You just need to know where to look. If you are new to how AI generates content in the first place, my [introduction to AI for beginners](/articles/introduction-to-ai-for-beginners) covers the fundamentals.

## How to spot AI-generated videos

Video deepfakes have improved dramatically, but they still struggle with certain physical details. Here is what I check, in order:

**Eyes and blinking.** Early deepfakes barely blinked at all. Modern ones blink more naturally, but the rate is often wrong. Real humans blink 15-20 times per minute. Count the blinks in a 30-second clip. Too few or too regular? Suspicious. Also check for eye reflections. In a real video, the light source reflects in both eyes at the same angle. Deepfakes sometimes get this wrong, with the reflection landing at slightly different positions in each eye.

**Ears and hairline.** Generative models still struggle with ears. Look for asymmetry, unusual shapes, or ears that seem to change size between frames. The hairline is another weak spot. Real hair has flyaways and imperfections. Deepfake hair often looks too smooth or blends unnaturally into the forehead.

**Lighting and shadows.** Does the shadow on the person's face match the light sources in the room? AI often places a face onto a body without matching the lighting conditions of the background. If the left side of the face is lit but the ambient light comes from the right, something is off.

**Background jitter.** Watch the area immediately around the person's face and neck. Deepfake algorithms sometimes cause the background to warp or shimmer near the edges of the face mask. This is especially visible around the jawline and chin.

**Mouth and teeth.** Lip-sync errors are common. The mouth movements may lag slightly behind the audio, or the teeth may look blurred, too uniform, or like a single white block instead of individual teeth.

**Hands near the face.** When a person's hand passes in front of their face, deepfakes often glitch. The hand may distort, merge with the face, or flicker. Real life does not do that.

A quick tip: pause the video at random moments and look closely. Deepfakes tend to look worst in single frozen frames, even when the motion seems smooth.

## How to detect AI voice clones

Audio deepfakes are arguably more dangerous than video ones because most scam calls happen over the phone, where you cannot see the person. A 2025 McAfee survey found that 1 in 4 people globally had received a call that used a cloned voice. Here is what to listen for.

**Flat emotional tone.** AI voice clones can replicate words and cadence, but they often miss the subtle emotional shifts in real speech. If your "family member" is calling about an emergency but sounds oddly calm or monotone, that is a red flag.

**Breathing patterns.** Real humans breathe at natural pauses in speech. Cloned voices sometimes skip breaths entirely or place them in unnatural spots. Listen for long stretches of speech with no audible breathing.

**Background noise.** In a real phone call, background noise is consistent and natural. AI-generated calls sometimes have no background noise at all (unnervingly clean) or have a looping, repetitive noise pattern that sounds artificial.

**Unusual phrasing.** Even if the voice sounds right, the script may not. AI clones trained on limited data may use phrases the real person would never say. If your boss calls and uses formal language they normally avoid, pause and verify.

**Call me back trick.** This is the simplest defense. If you get a suspicious call from a family member or colleague, hang up and call them back on the number you already have. A cloned voice cannot answer a call to the real person's phone.

## AI text and image deepfakes

Deepfakes are not limited to video and audio. AI-generated text and images are also part of the problem.

**AI-generated text.** Articles, emails, and social media posts written by AI often share certain patterns:

- Overly balanced structure. Every paragraph is roughly the same length. Real human writing is messier.
- Generic filler phrases. "It is important to note that..." and "In today's fast-paced world..." are classic AI tells.
- Lack of specific personal details. AI text speaks in generalities. A human writer will include personal anecdotes, specific dates, and named sources.
- Unusual word choices. AI sometimes picks a synonym that is technically correct but sounds off in context. "Utilize" where a human would say "use," for example.

Of course, AI detection tools are not perfect. The better approach is to cross-check claims with trusted sources rather than relying on a detector to flag the text for you.

**AI-generated images.** Despite huge progress, AI images still have recurring problems:

- **Hands.** Extra fingers, merged fingers, or fingers bending at impossible angles. This has improved since 2024, but it remains the single most common flaw.
- **Text in the image.** AI models generate letter-like shapes but often produce gibberish. Check any signs, labels, or book covers in the image. If the text does not make sense, the image is likely AI-generated.
- **Symmetry of accessories.** Earrings that do not match, glasses with different arm thickness, or buttons that are slightly off-center.
- **Background logic.** A staircase that leads to nowhere. A window that is not aligned with the wall. AI generates locally plausible details that break down at the architectural level.

## Real-world deepfake scams to watch out for

Deepfakes are not theoretical threats. These scams are happening right now.

**Investment fraud.** Fake celebrity endorsement videos are a massive problem. In 2025, a deepfake video of a well-known financial influencer promoting a crypto platform scammed victims out of an estimated $14 million before it was taken down. These videos often run as social media ads. The celebrity appears to endorse a specific platform, urging viewers to invest immediately. If you see a celebrity promoting an investment on a video ad, verify it through the celebrity's official channels before doing anything.

**Kidnapping and emergency scams.** This one is terrifying. A scammer clones your child's voice, calls you, and claims to have been kidnapped. The voice is convincing. The "child" is crying. The scammer demands immediate payment. Variations include fake car accidents, arrests, and medical emergencies. The common thread: urgency and a demand for money right now. Always hang up and call the person directly.

**Political manipulation.** Deepfake videos of politicians making inflammatory statements have circulated in at least 14 countries during recent elections. In some cases, the deepfakes were released within 24 hours of voting, leaving no time for fact-checkers to respond. Be skeptical of any politically charged video that appears in the final days before an election, especially if it is shared without a link to a reputable news source.

**Romance and social engineering.** AI-generated profile photos are now standard in romance scams. Video calls can be faked in real-time using face-swap tools. If someone you have met only online refuses to meet in person, asks for money, or seems too good to be true, proceed with extreme caution.

## Tools that help you detect deepfakes

You do not have to rely on your eyes and ears alone. These tools can help.

**Microsoft Video Authenticator.** This tool analyzes a video and provides a confidence score on whether it has been manipulated. It looks for blending boundaries and subtle pixel-level artifacts that the human eye misses. Available for media organizations and public figures, with a consumer version in development.

**Hive Moderation.** Originally built for content platforms, Hive offers a free browser-based tool where you can upload an image or video to check for AI generation. It is one of the most accurate detectors available, with reported accuracy above 95% for images.

**Illuminarty.** A free tool focused on AI-generated images. Upload a photo and it highlights the areas most likely to be AI-generated, along with an overall probability score. It works well for Stable Diffusion and Midjourney outputs in particular.

**AI Voice Detector.** Several browser-based tools can analyze audio clips for signs of voice synthesis. Resemble AI's Detect tool and Pindrop's audio analysis platform both offer free tiers. Upload a short audio clip and get a probability assessment.

**Reverse image search.** Sometimes the simplest tool works best. If a suspicious image looks like a news photo, drag it into Google Images or TinEye. If it only appears on a handful of random websites with no credible news coverage, treat it as unverified.

**A note of caution.** No detection tool is 100% reliable. They produce false positives and false negatives. Use them as one input in your judgment, not the final word.

## What to do if you encounter a deepfake

Finding a deepfake can be unsettling, especially if it involves you or someone you know. Here is a concrete action plan.

**1. Do not share it.** Every share amplifies the damage. Even sharing it with a "this is fake" caption can spread it further.

**2. Save the evidence.** Take a screenshot or screen recording of the deepfake, including the URL, the account that posted it, and the date. If it is a video, screen-record it before it gets deleted. This documentation is important for any report you file.

**3. Report it to the platform.** Every major social media platform now has a specific reporting path for AI-manipulated media. Use it. Platforms respond faster to deepfake reports than to general content complaints.

**4. Report it to authorities.** In the US, file a report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3). In the UK, use Action Fraud. In the EU, contact your national cybercrime unit. If the deepfake is being used for fraud, local law enforcement should also be involved.

**5. Contact a lawyer if you are the subject.** If a deepfake uses your likeness without consent, you may have legal options. Several US states and EU countries have passed specific deepfake laws. The legal situation is evolving fast, so consult someone who specializes in digital media law.

**6. Tell people in your circle.** If a deepfake of you is circulating, warn your contacts before they see it. A quick message saying "a fake video of me is going around, it is not real" can prevent a lot of confusion and damage.

## FAQ

**Can deepfakes be detected by the human eye?**
Often, yes, if you know what to look for. The tips in this article (eye reflections, ear shapes, lighting mismatches, background jitter) catch the majority of consumer-grade deepfakes. Professional-grade deepfakes made by skilled editors are harder to spot and may require detection tools.

**Is it illegal to create a deepfake?**
It depends on your jurisdiction and the intent. In many US states, creating deepfake pornography without consent is illegal. Several countries have laws against deepfakes used for fraud or election interference. Creating a deepfake for personal entertainment in a private context may not be illegal in many places, but this is an area where laws are changing rapidly.

**How long does it take to make a deepfake?**
A basic face-swap video can be made in 10-15 minutes with free tools on a standard computer. A high-quality voice clone needs about 30 seconds of clear audio. More sophisticated deepfakes that withstand scrutiny take longer and require more skill, but the floor is very low.

**Can AI deepfake detectors be wrong?**
Yes. All detection tools produce both false positives (flagging real media as fake) and false negatives (missing actual deepfakes). Accuracy rates for the best tools hover around 90-95% for images, lower for video, and even lower for audio. Treat detection tools as helpful but not infallible.

**What is the difference between a deepfake and regular photo editing?**
Regular photo editing (adjusting brightness, removing a background, retouching skin) has been around for decades. Deepfakes specifically use AI to replace or generate faces, voices, or entire bodies in a way that is meant to deceive. The key distinction is intent and the use of generative AI models.

**Should I be worried about deepfakes targeting me personally?**
If you have a public online presence (social media photos, YouTube videos, podcast appearances), your face and voice data are already available. The risk is higher for public figures, but ordinary people are increasingly targeted in financial scams and harassment campaigns. Basic awareness of the detection tips in this guide goes a long way.
