Cerebras Systems went public on May 14 and put on a show. The AI chip maker priced at $185 per share, opened at $350, hit $385 before a trading halt, and closed at $311.07, a 68% gain. By day two, it had slipped to $279.72, down 10%. Welcome to the public markets.

The $5.55 billion raise makes it the largest U.S. tech IPO since Uber in 2019. With the greenshoe option fully exercised, that number climbs past $6.3 billion. At the day-one close, Cerebras was worth about $68 billion.

Those are the numbers. The question is what they mean.

## What Cerebras Actually Makes

Cerebras builds the WSE-3 (Wafer Scale Engine), a single chip about 58 times larger than NVIDIA's B200. Where NVIDIA strings thousands of smaller chips together, Cerebras packs an entire cluster's compute onto one piece of silicon.

The upside: no communication bottlenecks between chips. For AI inference — the process of running a trained model to generate responses — Cerebras claims 15x speedups over comparable GPU setups. At least one very large customer is taking that claim seriously.

OpenAI signed a multiyear deal with Cerebras worth over $10 billion for compute capacity, plus a $20 billion chip purchase agreement. An endorsement like that turns a speculative hardware bet into a real business.

The downside hasn't changed: every AI framework, library, and tool is built for NVIDIA first. Cerebras means rewriting or adapting software to its platform. That friction is the single biggest risk to the bull case.

## The Actual Financials

Cerebras is one of the rare AI hardware companies already turning a profit. Revenue for 2025 hit $510 million, up 76% year over year, with net income around $238 million — a 47% net margin. Three years ago, it booked $24 million in revenue.

The growth line is steep and real. The question for public market investors is whether the multiple they're paying, about 133x trailing earnings at the day-one close, prices in enough growth to leave room for upside.

## The NVIDIA Question, Post-IPO

NVIDIA's market cap sits above $5 trillion. Every major AI lab builds on its hardware. The data center business keeps growing faster than analysts can revise their models. Lead times for H200 clusters still stretch past 12 months.

Cerebras isn't trying to dethrone NVIDIA. It's going after a specific slice of the market where its architecture has a real advantage: running the largest models at inference time, where communication overhead between GPUs becomes the bottleneck. The OpenAI deal shows the advantage is real.

The bull case: demand for AI compute is growing so fast that even a distant second place becomes a massive business. Cloud providers and large enterprises want alternatives to NVIDIA, if only for pricing leverage. Cerebras doesn't need to win the whole market.

The bear case hasn't changed either: the CUDA software moat has killed every challenger before. Custom architecture means custom engineering investment from customers. That's a tough sell when "just buy more NVIDIA" is the safe choice.

## What the IPO Says About AI Hardware

Cerebras priced at $185 after originally marketing the deal at $150 to $160. Demand was 20x oversubscribed. They upsized multiple times and still popped 68% on the open.

For a hardware company, in a market that's been cautious through early 2026, that level of demand tells you something. The market thinks the next phase of AI returns will be won in silicon, not software.

The first wave of AI investment went into foundation model companies. The second is going into picks and shovels: the chips, data centers, and networking equipment that make AI physically possible. Cerebras, AMD's recent earnings beat, and the massive capex numbers from Microsoft, Google, and Meta all point the same direction.

The first-week volatility — up 68%, then down 10% — is normal for a debut this size. The real test comes over the next two quarters: can Cerebras execute on the OpenAI deal, grow revenue at the pace its multiple demands, and prove that its architecture holds a lasting place in an industry built on someone else's standard.
