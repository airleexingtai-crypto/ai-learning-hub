## A Chip Shortage Nobody Is Calling a Shortage Yet

NVIDIA is shipping Blackwell Ultra GPUs as fast as it can make them. Cloud providers are buying them as fast as NVIDIA can ship them. The result is a 3.6 million unit backlog that stretches through the middle of 2026, with 60,000 GB300 NVL72 racks projected to ship this year, up 129% from last year's numbers.

Nobody is calling this a shortage yet because the chips are moving. But if you are waiting for AI inference costs to drop because of new hardware, the supply side of that equation is running into the limits of physics and factory capacity.

## The Numbers Behind the Backlog

The GB300 NVL72 rack, the configuration most hyperscale customers want, packs 36 Grace CPUs and 72 Blackwell Ultra GPUs into a single liquid-cooled unit. Each rack costs around $3 million. Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta have placed orders that will take most of 2026 to fulfill.

The performance jump from the previous generation is real. Each Blackwell Ultra GPU delivers about 50% more throughput than a B200 on inference workloads. Compared to the H100, the chip most AI companies were using two years ago, the uplift is roughly 30x. That is not a marginal improvement. That is the kind of generational leap that rewrites what is economically feasible to run.

The problem is that demand is growing faster than the performance improvement. AI model inference volume is not just doubling. It is more than doubling, driven by reasoning models that need thousands of tokens of internal chain-of-thought before they produce a single word of output for the user.

## Why Inference Costs Are Not Dropping Yet

The math should be simple. Faster chips equal cheaper inference. That logic works in a world where demand stays constant.

Demand is not staying constant. Agentic AI, models that browse the web, run code, query databases, and coordinate with other agents, consumes an order of magnitude more inference than a single chatbot query. A user asking ChatGPT a question might burn a few hundred tokens. An AI agent planning a multi-step task, checking its own work, spawning sub-agents, and iterating across failures might burn tens of thousands of tokens before the user sees a result.

NVIDIA's Dynamo inference framework, announced at GTC 2025, is designed to manage this explosion. It orchestrates inference across thousands of GPUs, distributing reasoning workloads in ways that maximize throughput. It is open source. It is also a signal that NVIDIA sees the same trend everyone else sees. Inference demand is going vertical, and throwing more chips at the problem is the only near-term solution.

## Vera Rubin Is Coming, But Not Soon Enough

NVIDIA's next architecture, Vera Rubin, is expected in the second half of 2026. It brings NVLink 6 at 3,600 Gbps, HBM4 memory at 13 Tbps, and a Rubin Ultra configuration that scales to 576 GPUs per rack. The numbers are staggering.

But Vera Rubin will not relieve supply pressure immediately. Hyperscale customers will take every chip NVIDIA can manufacture, Blackwell Ultra now, Vera Rubin later, and the backlog will likely persist through 2027. AI companies that do not have multi-billion-dollar infrastructure budgets are left renting from the cloud providers, paying whatever the market rate happens to be on a given day.

## The Price You Actually Pay

For most people, Blackwell Ultra availability is an abstraction. You do not order a GPU rack. You pay $20 a month for ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro, and the inference cost is someone else's problem.

But those subscriptions are priced on a bet that inference gets cheaper over time. If the hardware supply cannot keep up with demand, that bet looks shakier. We are already seeing the pressure in [the AI industry's broader economics](/blog/ai-industry-money-problem). Subscription prices that made sense when inference cost pennies per query make less sense when each query spawns an agent that runs for 10 minutes.

The chip shortage is real. It just has not reached your wallet yet.
